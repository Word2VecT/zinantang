Introduction

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is only as good as its data. Two families of methods try to improve that data. Data selection filters a pool with heuristics or statistics, such as response length1 or Instruction-Following Difficulty2, and keeps the samples that score well. Data synthesis asks a stronger LLM to write new samples3. Both are one-off. Selection applies fixed criteria that ignore what the model has already learned, and synthesis often discards the original data and can produce narrow or redundant samples.

We introduce Middo, model-informed dynamic data optimization. Middo puts the dataset and the model in a closed loop: the model being fine-tuned diagnoses its own training data, the samples it flags are rewritten rather than dropped, and the refined data trains the next model. The diagnosis uses three signals read from the model. Loss patterns find samples that are too complex for it, embedding cluster dynamics find sparse regions of its representation space, and self-alignment scores find samples it judges to be low quality.

On Alpaca with LLaMA-3.1-8B, three rounds of Middo raise the average over eight benchmarks from 32.48 to 39.63 (+7.15), while the dataset grows only from 52.0K to 57.6K samples. MATH rises from 4.96 to 12.92 and GPQA from 5.56 to 16.67. The gains hold on Mistral-7B-v0.3 and on stronger seed data (Figure 3).

Method

Middo repeats one loop: train a model on the current data, read three signals from it, select the samples each signal flags, refine them with an LLM, and write them back.

The closed loop

Key idea 1The model that will learn from the data decides which data needs work.

Let $\mathcal{D}_t$ be the training set at iteration $t$, with $\mathcal{D}_0$ the seed set. Each iteration fine-tunes the base model for one epoch on $\mathcal{D}_t$, giving $\theta_t$; training from the base model each time, rather than continuing from $\theta_{t-1}$, avoids overfitting to data the model has already seen4. $\theta_t$ then reads every sample, the three selectors return $\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hard}}$, $\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sparse}}$, and $\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{low}}$, and an LLM refines each into its primed counterpart:

$$\mathcal{D}_{t+1}=\Big(\mathcal{D}_t\setminus\big(\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hard}}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{low}}\big)\Big)\cup\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hard}\prime}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{low}\prime}\cup\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sparse}\prime}$$

Hard and low-quality samples are replaced, so they do not change the size of the set. Sparse samples are kept and extended, so the set grows slightly: Alpaca goes from 52,002 to 57,636 samples over three iterations. Each selector cuts at a dynamic threshold relative to the current distribution of its signal $s$:

$$\tau=\mu_s+m\,\sigma_s$$

where $\mu_s$ and $\sigma_s$ are the mean and standard deviation of the signal over $\mathcal{D}_t$, and $m$ sets how strict the cut is. With $m$ fixed, the flagged set shrinks as the data improves: on Alpaca with LLaMA-3.1-8B, loss patterns flag 1,180 samples in iteration 1, 299 in iteration 2, and 242 in iteration 3.

Loss patterns: complexity

Key idea 2A sample the model still cannot fit after training is too hard for it now. Simplify it; do not throw it away.

For a sample $(X_i,Y_i)$, the SFT loss is the negative log-likelihood of the response given the instruction. Middo compares it before and after training, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pre}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{post}}$. Low on both means easy. High on both means the sample stayed hard through training, a mismatch between its complexity and the model’s ability:

$$\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{hard}}=\big\{(X_i,Y_i):\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pre}}(X_i,Y_i)>\tau_{\mathrm{pre}}\ \wedge\ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{post}}(X_i,Y_i)>\tau_{\mathrm{post}}\big\}$$

For each hard sample, an LLM lists ways to make it easier to learn, plans a rewrite, and simplifies it step by step while keeping what it teaches, in the style of automatic instruction evolving5. The simplified sample replaces the original (Figure 2).

Embedding cluster dynamics: diversity

Key idea 3A sample with no close neighbors marks a gap in coverage. Write new data around it.

Middo embeds each sample with the last hidden layer $\mathcal{H}^{(L)}$ of $\theta_t$, mean-pooled over tokens, and finds its $k$ nearest neighbors $\mathcal{N}_k(X_i)$ by cosine similarity. The diversity score is the mean similarity to those neighbors:

$$d(X_i)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{X_j\in\mathcal{N}_k(X_i)}\cos\big(h(X_i),\,h(X_j)\big)$$

A low score means the sample lies in a sparse region. Samples below the threshold form $\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{sparse}}$. For each one, the LLM receives the sample as the core prompt and its neighbors as hints, and writes one new sample. The new samples are added and the originals stay, so the distribution widens. $k=2$ works best (Choosing m and k).

Self-alignment scores: quality

Key idea 4No external judge. The model being trained grades its own data.

LLM-as-a-judge filters such as AlpaGasus6 rely on a strong external model. Middo asks $\theta_t$ itself. It scores the instruction alone, $S^{\mathrm{ins}}_{\pi}(X_i)$, and the instruction with its response, $S^{\mathrm{res}}_{\pi}(X_i,Y_i)$, from 0 to 10 on three AlignBench7 dimensions $\pi$: clarity, completeness, and factuality. The quality score averages the six:

$$S(X_i,Y_i)=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{\pi}\Big(S^{\mathrm{ins}}_{\pi}(X_i)+S^{\mathrm{res}}_{\pi}(X_i,Y_i)\Big),\qquad \pi\in\{\text{clarity},\ \text{completeness},\ \text{factuality}\}$$

Samples below the threshold form $\mathcal{D}^{\mathrm{low}}$. The LLM diagnoses why each one is weak, plans a fix, and rewrites it, and the rewrite replaces the original.

What a refinement looks like

Choosing m and k

The amount refined per selector is set by $m$ in $\tau=\mu+m\sigma$. Loss patterns flag the high tail, so their $m$ is positive; diversity and quality flag the low tail, so theirs is negative. A sweep on the first iteration of Alpaca gives the best average when about 15% of samples are selected in total (Table 2). We target 10–20% and, among the settings in that range, use the mildest thresholds.

The number of neighbors matters too. With $k=2$ the average is 35.72, against 33.30 for $k=1$ and 32.95 for $k=3$: two neighbors expand the data enough to help generalization without adding noise.

Experiments

Setup

  • Seed data. Alpaca8 (52K); Alpaca-4o-mini, the same instructions with responses regenerated by GPT-4o-mini, so that gains cannot come from 4o-mini outputs alone; and WizardLM9 (70K). Each runs three iterations of Middo.
  • Refiner. GPT-4o-mini through DataDreamer10, with temperature and top-p at 1.0 for diversity. No stronger model is needed.
  • Training. LLaMA-3.1-8B11 and Mistral-7B-v0.312 with LLaMA-Factory13, one epoch per iteration from the base model, learning rate $2\times10^{-5}$ for LLaMA and $1\times10^{-5}$ for Mistral, cosine schedule.
  • Evaluation. OpenCompass14 with vLLM15, zero-shot: MMLU and IFEval (general), GSM8K and MATH (math), HumanEval and MBPP (code), HellaSwag and GPQA (reasoning).

Main results

Finding 1Every seed set, both models: the refined data beats the data it came from, by up to 7.15 points.

Figure 3 compares each seed set with its three Middo iterations. The weakest seed set gains most: Alpaca rises 7.15 points on LLaMA-3.1-8B and 4.75 on Mistral-7B-v0.3. On LLaMA, GSM8K rises by 15.55 and HellaSwag by 11.11; on Mistral, MMLU rises by 11.07, GSM8K by 12.59, and GPQA by 10.60.

Two controls. Alpaca-4o-mini starts from GPT-4o-mini responses, the same model Middo refines with, and still gains 2.20 points on LLaMA (MMLU +11.87), so the gain is not 4o-mini’s style alone. And better seed data needs fewer rounds: on LLaMA, Alpaca peaks at iteration 3, Alpaca-4o-mini at iteration 2, and WizardLM at iteration 1.

Table 3. Results by benchmark. init trains on the seed data; iter n trains on the data after n rounds of Middo. Every row is one epoch of SFT from the base model. Orange: best average for that seed set; underlined: second best.

Against selection and augmentation

Finding 2Refining beats filtering and beats writing from scratch. The 8.8K refined samples alone score 42.60, above every baseline.

We compare on Alpaca with LLaMA-3.1-8B against data selection, Alpaca-cleaned16, Superfiltering17, Long1, and AlpaGasus6, and against data augmentation, Alpaca-GPT43, I-SHEEP4, and WizardLM9. Selection methods train on a subset, so for a like-for-like comparison MiddOnly trains only on the samples Middo produced, without the untouched ones.

Middo on Alpaca-4o-mini has the best average, 42.96. MiddOnly Alpaca, with 8.8K samples, reaches 42.60, ahead of WizardLM’s 38.96 with 70K: what drives the gain is which samples are rewritten, not how many there are. Full Middo Alpaca, at 39.63, also beats every baseline.

Ablation

Removing any one selector lowers the average at every iteration. w/o loss drops loss patterns, w/o neighbor drops embedding cluster dynamics, and w/o score drops self-alignment scores. Which loss hurts most changes over the iterations: without self-alignment scores the first iteration falls from 38.25 to 34.67, and by the second and third, dropping embedding clusters costs the most (39.01 to 35.89 in the third). The three signals are complementary: complexity, diversity, and quality each need their own selector.

Analysis

How much to refine

Finding 3Refine a slice, not the whole set. Performance peaks with about 15% of samples refined and falls after that.

We vary the thresholds to change how many samples are selected and record the average score over three iterations. Both models rise and then fall: LLaMA-3.1-8B peaks at 39.62 with about 7.7K samples (14.9% of Alpaca), and Mistral-7B-v0.3 at 30.90 with about 6.9K (13.3%). A moderate amount also leaves room for later iterations, so Middo selects about 5% per selector in the first iteration and lets the dynamic thresholds shrink the selection after that. The number of iterations behaves the same way: on Alpaca, the average climbs for three iterations and falls in the fourth, to about 36.7.

Complexity: the loss tail is cut

Finding 4The data moves toward the model. The loss tail is cut, sparse regions fill in, and self-scores rise.

Figure 5 compares the per-sample loss of the original data with the data after Middo. The original distribution has a long tail, up to a maximum loss of 12.99. After Middo the maximum is 3.76, 71.05% lower, and density moves from the tail into the body of the distribution: the samples that were too complex for the model are gone, and the rest is easier for it to fit.

Diversity: the gaps fill in

Figure 6 projects Alpaca with t-SNE18. The samples Middo adds sit mostly at the edges of clusters and in sparsely populated regions, not on top of existing data. They widen coverage of the model’s representation space rather than duplicating it.

Quality: self-scores rise

Figure 7 tracks the mean self-alignment score of each dataset across iterations. Alpaca rises from 7.02 to 8.06 and WizardLM from 7.48 to 8.60. Alpaca-4o-mini starts high and stays near 8.0. As low-quality samples are rewritten, the data converges toward what the model itself judges clear, complete, and factual.

Cost

One iteration, selection plus refinement, finishes in under 30 minutes on 8 × A100 GPUs for datasets of 50K–100K samples. Neighbor search runs on CUDA and self-scoring uses batched vLLM inference.

Conclusion

Middo turns SFT data curation from a one-off filter into a loop in which data and model improve together. Three signals read from the model being trained, loss patterns, embedding cluster dynamics, and self-alignment scores, find samples that are too complex, under-covered, or low quality, and an LLM rewrites them instead of discarding them. Across two models and three seed sets, the refined data beats the original at nearly the same size, by up to 7.15 points on average.

Limitations. The diagnostics come from the model being trained, so a weak base model gives weak signals. Middo does not yet use reinforcement learning, the loop adds compute as data grows or updates more often, and biases in the seed data can carry through refinement.
